Exploring the Red Planet

Mars has fascinated humanity for centuries, and the last few decades have turned that fascination into serious exploration. The planet is a cold desert world with a thin carbon dioxide atmosphere, rusty iron-oxide soil, and the largest volcano in the solar system, Olympus Mons.

Robotic missions have led the way. Orbiters map the surface and monitor weather from above, while landers and rovers study the ground directly. NASA's Curiosity and Perseverance rovers roll across ancient lakebeds, drilling into rock and analyzing samples for signs that Mars was once habitable. Perseverance even carried a small helicopter, Ingenuity, which proved that powered flight is possible in the planet's wispy air.

One of the biggest scientific questions is water. Dry riverbeds, mineral deposits, and buried ice all suggest that liquid water once flowed on the surface billions of years ago. Where there was water, there may once have been the conditions for life, which is why the search for ancient microbes drives so much of the science.

Sending humans is the next frontier. A crewed mission faces enormous challenges: radiation during the long journey, the need to grow food and recycle air, and the difficulty of landing heavy spacecraft safely. Engineers are testing reusable rockets and life-support systems to make a round trip to Mars feasible within our lifetime.
